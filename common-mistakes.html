Common mistakes new developers make on site work projects

Most costly surprises on earthwork and utility projects trace back to a handful of avoidable mistakes. Here's what we see most often in the Las Vegas Valley.

1. Skipping or rushing the geotechnical report

It's tempting to save money by skipping a soils report or reusing one from a nearby parcel. But Las Vegas Valley soil conditions — caliche layers, variable rock, and localized expansive clay — can differ significantly within short distances. A cheap or missing geotech report is one of the fastest ways to end up with a change order once excavation starts.

2. Assuming the general contractor "handles" site work

Earthwork and underground utilities are typically a specialized scope, often bid and contracted separately from vertical construction. Developers who assume their GC has this fully scoped sometimes discover — late — that grading or utility work was never actually included in that contract, leading to schedule gaps and budget surprises.

3. Underestimating permitting timelines

Because a single project can touch a city or county building department and a separate water or sewer district, permitting can take longer than developers expect — especially if plans need revisions after first review. Submitting for permits later than planned is one of the most common causes of a delayed groundbreaking. See our permitting overview for what to expect.

4. Choosing a contractor on price alone

Low bids on earthwork and utility scopes often mean something is excluded — haul-off, dewatering, rock excavation, traffic control, or full compaction testing. A bid that looks like a great deal can end up costing more once those items surface as change orders. Comparing scope of work line by line matters more than comparing the bottom-line number. Our contractor checklist covers what to look for.

5. Not accounting for cut/fill balance early

Civil plans that require large amounts of import or export soil can add significant, sometimes unbudgeted, hauling costs. Reviewing grading plans for cut/fill balance during design — not after bids come in — gives you a chance to adjust the design and control cost.

6. Ignoring storm drainage requirements

Because of the Las Vegas Valley's flash-flood risk during summer monsoon storms, drainage design is closely reviewed by local jurisdictions and the Clark County Regional Flood Control District. Developers who treat storm drain as an afterthought — rather than designing for it alongside grading — often face plan revisions and delays during review.

7. Not verifying licensing and insurance before signing

Nevada requires state contractor licensing (through the Nevada State Contractors Board) for this type of work, and different license classifications apply to different scopes. Hiring an unlicensed or improperly classified contractor can create real liability exposure and jeopardize your ability to get permits finaled.

8. Poor sequencing between trades

Utilities, grading, and paving/landscaping all depend on each other's timing. Developers managing multiple contractors directly sometimes create sequencing conflicts — for example, landscaping starting before utility trenches are fully backfilled and tested. A single, experienced site contractor (or a well-coordinated construction manager) helps avoid this.

9. Not budgeting a contingency

Because underground conditions can't be fully known until you dig, even a well-planned project can hit unexpected rock, old debris, or utility conflicts. Developers who budget zero contingency on earthwork/utility scopes are the ones most likely to face a funding gap mid-project.

The pattern behind most of these mistakes

Nearly every item on this list comes down to the same root cause: treating site work as a minor line item instead of the foundation-setting phase it actually is. Getting good information and a properly vetted contractor early is the highest-leverage thing you can do.
