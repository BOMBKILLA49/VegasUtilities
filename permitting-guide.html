Permitting & inspections overview for the Las Vegas Valley

Site development permitting in Southern Nevada involves more than one agency. Here's a general map of who's typically involved and what the process looks like — not a substitute for confirming current requirements with each agency directly.

Requirements change and vary by jurisdiction

This page is a general educational overview, not legal or engineering advice. Exact requirements, fees, and review timelines vary by jurisdiction and change over time. Always confirm current requirements directly with the applicable building department or utility district, and rely on your civil engineer and licensed contractor to manage the actual permitting process.

Step one: figure out which jurisdiction applies

The Las Vegas Valley is served by several separate building departments, and which one governs your project depends on your property's exact location:

  • City of Las Vegas — Department of Building & Safety, for properties within Las Vegas city limits.
  • Clark County — Department of Building & Fire Prevention, for unincorporated Clark County (which includes many areas people casually think of as "Las Vegas," like Spring Valley, Summerlin, and Paradise).
  • City of Henderson — its own Building & Fire Safety Department for properties within Henderson city limits.
  • City of North Las Vegas — its own Development Services / Building Safety Department for properties within North Las Vegas city limits.

It's common for people to assume "Las Vegas" is one jurisdiction — in practice, your address determines which department reviews your grading and site work permits, and each has its own submittal process and review timeline.

Step two: identify the utility districts involved

Separate from the building department, your project will typically need approval from the agency that owns the utility infrastructure you're connecting to:

  • Water — commonly the Las Vegas Valley Water District (serving the City of Las Vegas and much of unincorporated Clark County), or the applicable city utility department for Henderson or North Las Vegas.
  • Sanitary sewer — commonly the Clark County Water Reclamation District, or a city's own utility department depending on location.
  • Storm drainage — reviewed against standards developed with the Clark County Regional Flood Control District, in addition to the local jurisdiction's own drainage requirements.

Larger sites, or sites near a jurisdictional boundary, sometimes touch more than one of these agencies at once — which is part of why early coordination matters.

What a typical permitting sequence looks like

  1. Civil plan preparation — a civil engineer prepares grading and utility plans based on your project's design and the geotechnical report.
  2. Plan submittal — plans are submitted to the relevant building department and utility district(s) for review.
  3. Plan review & comments — agencies review for compliance with grading, drainage, and utility standards; it's common to receive comments requiring revisions before approval.
  4. Permit issuance — once plans are approved, grading and utility permits are issued and work can begin.
  5. Construction inspections — inspectors typically visit at key milestones: subgrade/compaction, utility trench bedding and backfill, pressure/video testing, and final grading.
  6. Final acceptance — for utility lines that will be dedicated to a public agency (public sewer or water mains), that agency typically must formally accept the completed infrastructure.

What can slow a permit down

  • Incomplete or non-compliant civil plans requiring multiple review cycles
  • Missing or outdated geotechnical information
  • Unresolved conflicts with existing utilities shown (or not shown) on record drawings
  • High plan review volume at the agency during busy periods
  • Drainage designs that don't clearly demonstrate compliance with flood control standards

Nevada contractor licensing

Contractors performing earthwork, grading, and underground utility work in Nevada are generally required to hold an appropriate license classification issued by the Nevada State Contractors Board (NSCB). Verifying a contractor's license and classification is a basic first step before hiring — see our contractor checklist for how to do that.
